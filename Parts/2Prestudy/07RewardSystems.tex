\section{Reward Systems}
\label{chap:rewardsystems}
Reward systems play an essential role in every game. Players are motivated by rewards and reward systems are used as tools for increasing social meaning \citep{wang_game_2011}. Rewards are especially important in gamification applications, where the concept often is built around introducing reward systems to increase the users’ enjoyment. 

\citeauthor{wang_game_2011} presents an article describing how reward systems are used in games, and which design considerations a game developer should take into account. The article proposes classifications for rewards, reward characteristics, and how players utilize them. In this chapter, the key points in the article will be presented, including the aspects just mentioned. After that, a study conducted by \citeauthor{wang_effect_2016} illustrating the effect of game reward systems (points and audio) in Kahoot! will be presented.

\subsection{Forms of Rewards}
Based on multiple studies done by other researchers, \citeauthor{wang_game_2011} proposed eight forms of rewards used in games to create positive experiences for the players: 

\subsubsection{Score Systems}
Score systems are a type of reward that uses numbers to keep track of the player’s performance. Scores are considered crucial to the design of a fun game by many researchers and generally serve as a tool for self-assessment and comparison. The reward system is typically classified as a “glory” reward, i.e. it does not have a direct impact on gameplay. World of Warcraft is an example of a game where the score system reward is used. The ladder system uses scores to determine player ratings

\subsubsection{Experience Point Reward Systems}
Experience Points (XP) are utilized in the majority of games where players control developable avatars. XP are typically used to measure the players’ progress in the game, leveling up when a certain number of points is reached. The reward is classified as a “facility” type of reward, meaning a reward that increases the avatar skills at certain levels. XP is similar to score systems described above, but differ in at least three ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item XP are bound to specific avatars rather than the player itself.
    \item XP are rarely used for a ranking purpose.
    \item XP directly affects gameplay.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Item Granting System Rewards}
This type of reward consists of in-game items that can be used by players or avatars. Item granting systems are often adopted in RPGs and MMORPGs to encourage exploration of the game world. Their main purpose is normally to maintain players’ interest in-between intense battles, missions, or other exciting moments. 

\subsubsection{Resources}
Resources are in-game valuables collected and used in a way that affects the gameplay. The reward system is similar to an item granting system, but there is a main difference between the two: Resources are mostly for practical game use, while items have a collecting and social value only. An example of resources is virtual wood and stone in Age of Empires III.

\subsubsection{Achievement Systems}
Achievement systems consist of titles bound to a user or avatar collected by completing clearly stated requirements. This type of reward encourages players to complete certain tasks, which may be more challenging than what they otherwise would have considered doing. Achievements can also be a way of improving the players’ urge to explore the game world. As an example, there are over a thousand different titles in World of Warcraft. Collecting these is publicly acknowledged by the community, consequently encouraging players to complete these tasks.

\subsubsection{Feedback Messages}
Feedback messages are a way of providing instant feedback, an important element of flow \citep{sweetser_gameflow_2005}, to the player. The reward is not collectible and thus cannot be used as a player comparison or directly affect the gameplay. In Dance Dance Revolution a message with the word “Perfect” appears every time you perform the correct dance move with precise timing. This is a perfect example of a feedback message reward. The feedback can also be pictures, sound effects, or video clips.

\subsubsection{Plot Animations and Pictures}
Plot animations or pictures are used in games after important events, such as completing a level or a mission. The purpose of this reward is to motivate players to continue advancing in the game.

\subsubsection{Unlocking Mechanisms}
The last reward type is unlocking mechanisms, which gives players access to new game content when certain conditions are met. This could be a new level, a special environment, or similar. Unlocking mechanisms are hiding elements and awarding them to players as they progress in the game. The reward is important in regards to maintaining player curiosity. An example is found in World of Warcraft where avatars must achieve certain levels before being able to access higher level environments.

\subsection{Reward Characteristics}
After explaining the eight forms of rewards \citeauthor{wang_game_2011} presents four kinds of related characteristics. The article describes how these four characteristics can be used to analyze the influence of reward systems on different kinds of people.

The first one, social value, can be used for comparison of players or social interaction. Rewards in games make it easier for players to compare themselves against others. An example is a high score list. The player can compare their points to others, to see how they rank. Another good example is how hard-to-get items often draw attention. Avatars wearing such items often attract attention from other players in the game.

The next characteristic looks at “how rewards affect gameplay” \cite{wang_game_2011}. This could be in the form of no direct effect, to helping the player advance in the game, to provide new content. Using the player types from Bartle’s taxonomy, the article summarizes the effect reward has on different player types as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Achievers and Killers:} Most interested in accumulating rewards that are evidence of their advanced skills and do not care about the visual aspect.
    \item \textbf{Socializers:} Greater interest in rewards that can draw attention to them, e.g. hard to get items.
    \item \textbf{Explorers:} Similar to Achievers and Killers, but the motivation behind is gaining sufficient strength to explore game world details.
\end{itemize}
The other way around, \citeauthor{wang_game_2011} also mentions that it is possible to determine player styles by looking at which rewards they want.

The third attribute is “suitability for collection and review” \cite{wang_game_2011}. The logic behind this is that building a sense of accomplishment improves the ability to preserve game memories. Collecting rewards is also a tool for players to recognize other players with similar interests. An important design consideration, related to this attribute, is to showcase the rewards so they are easy to present and review in the game. The rewards could then strengthen the sense of collection and produce a feeling of completion and perfection among the players.

The last characteristic is “the time required to earn and/or receive a reward“ \cite{wang_game_2011}. In contrast to real-world rewards, e.g. job promotion, a game reward can be instantaneous or rewarded after a couple of hours of gaming. A player may, for example, get a new level as a reward after a couple of hours of play. The time required to earn a reward is a crucial aspect of game design that needs to be carefully planned to create a positive player experience.

\subsection{Reward Systems Design Consideration}
The article proposes seven reward system design considerations, which are directly relevant for us when we are going to develop our alternative game concept:

\subsubsection{Life Constraint}
Rewards must be available in a manner that fits the intended target group of the game. Hardcore gamers tend to spend more time playing, thus the rewards should take longer to obtain. However, if the game is designed for casual gamers, the rewards should be accessible during shorter sessions.

\subsubsection{Create Autotelic Experiences}
The rewards should create intrinsically rewarding experiences. One way of doing this is to create multi-level goals which will keep them engaged in the game to a larger degree. Players will then also learn and experience the pleasure of learning. 

\subsubsection{Balance}
An essential part of designing reward systems is to balance the time spent with the value of the reward. It is frustrating for players to always feel like the rewards are not valuable enough. On the other hand, if the rewards are too valuable, the player might appreciate them less. 

\subsubsection{Uncertainty and Secrecy}
Uncertainty in rewards can create additional fun in games, but appropriate handling is essential. Not all reward types are suited for secrecy. As an example, ammunition and life counts are typical rewards that are preferred to be exposed to the player. Other types of rewards such as treasures and quests may be hidden to introduce more fun. Hiding these elements encourages players to dive deeper into the game and spend more time exploring it. 

\subsubsection{Accumulated vs. Instant Feedback}
Accumulated rewards are important attributes for marking progress and comparing status between players. Accumulated feedback is appropriate for creating long term and social sense of achievement. Contrary, instant feedback provides responsiveness and an element of flow to the game.

\subsubsection{Social Purposes}
Game rewards enable social status that can be shared with others. Hard to get rewards, such as special items, distinguishes advanced and casual players. These types of rewards also serve as a symbol to increase unity between skilled players.

\subsubsection{Physical World Activities}
Reward systems that support physical world activities impact the playing willingness. By being physically active, the guiltiness often associated with playing diminishes. An example is the physical world activities in Nintendo Wii, which changed the image of game playing. 

\subsection{The Effect of Points and Audio in Kahoot!}
To conclude the topic of rewards systems in games, a concrete example of its importance will be briefly described. \citeauthor{wang_effect_2016} conducted a study in \citeyear{wang_effect_2016} looking at the effects of points and audio in the game-based learning system Kahoot!. Points as a reward system correspond to the “score systems” category, while audio classifies as “feedback messages”, according to the forms of rewards presented earlier in this chapter.  

The paper describes an experiment where Kahoot! was used as a learning method for teaching a lecture on software engineering. The students were divided into four groups using four different combinations of reward systems. The first group used Kahoot! as normal, with sound and audio. The second group played the game with audio but without points. The third group played Kahoot! without audio but with points, and the last group without audio and points. After completing the lectures, a questionnaire with statements related to concentration, engagement, enjoyment, learning, and motivation was asked to be filled out by the students. 

The results from the study revealed notable differences in how the game was experienced in different groups. The effects of points and audio demonstrated a significant change in concentration, enjoyment, motivation, and engagement among the students. Audio had a prominent effect on classroom dynamics, but points also showed to contribute—to a more limited extent. An interesting observation was done in the group using Kahoot! where audio was removed but points still present. The classroom was completely silent during the lecture, and students reported afterward that it felt more like a formal test than a game \citep{wang_effect_2016}.

The study illustrates the importance of reward systems in a game. Without points and audio in Kahoot! several of the students answered that they were less motivated and engaged.

\subsection{Summary}
In this chapter, a study of reward systems and their importance in games has been introduced. This included the eight forms of reward systems in games, characteristics, and seven design guidelines. At last, a concrete example of the effects of reward systems in Kahoot! was presented. The topics considered in this chapter are highly relevant to us, later when we are going to develop our alternative game concept.
