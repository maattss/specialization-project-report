\section{What are alternative games?}
To be able to come up with a concept for an alternative game, as described in the research goal, a clear understanding of which games that can be categorized as alternative games is crucial. Let's first take a look at what “alternative” means in this context with some definitions of the word from three common dictionaries:

\textbf{Oxford Learner’s dictionary:} \blockquote{Different from the usual or traditional way in which something is done.} \citeyearpar{oxfords_learner_dictionary_alternative_2020}.

\textbf{Merriam Webster:} \blockquote{Different from the usual or conventional.} \citeyearpar{merriam_webster_alternative_2020}.
 
\textbf{Cambridge Dictionary:} \blockquote{Alternative things are considered to be unusual and often have a small but enthusiastic group of people who support them.} \citeyearpar{cambridge_dictionary_alternative_2020}.

While all of these dictionaries define the word differently, we can see that there are some commonalities. All three describe alternatives as something unusual or different from the usual. This may be the key take away from these definitions; an alternative game is a game where the creators have done something in such a way that it differs from other games.

To be able to more easily recognize alternative game concepts, we have looked at a lot of different existing alternative games and tried to find out what they have in common. The result of this research is a list of six characteristics, where alternative games often differ from ordinary ones. 

\subsection{Alternative game characteristics}
Our proposal of which games can be considered alternative games is then the following:

If a game deviates from regular games in one or more of the game characteristics categories mentioned below, it can be considered an alternative game. 

Below are explanations of all categories with coherent relevant alternative games presented.

These categories are covering all alternative games we have studied during this phase of the project. However, there might exist additional characteristics from games that we are not aware of, or that’s not developed yet. As technology moves forward, it’s reasonable to assume that new branches of alternative games might emerge.

\subsubsection{Input}
A common way of creating an alternative game is to use an unusual input source for controlling the game. This could be by using the position, microphone, special controllers, accelerometer, or something else. Some examples of games utilizing alternative input sources are Knowledge War \citep{wang_knowledge_2016} and CityZombie \citep{zhu_experiences_2010} which both use the players’ position as a central part of playing the game. Exermon \citep{wang_evaluation_2017}, an exercise game, is another example. This game utilizes the accelerometer to register how many repetitions of a strength exercise the user performs correctly.

\subsubsection{Output}
Output from the game is another characteristic commonly explored in alternative games. Some known examples are sound, haptics, and augmented reality, but there exists a lot more. Unexceptional.net is a game which uses an uncommon output method. It includes a lot of untraditional outputs, such as SMS and phone calls as ways of providing feedback to the player \citep{nideffer_unexceptional_2009}. Another game example is “A pervasive game to know your city better” which utilizes location and augmented reality to show different important landmarks in the area you are located where you are \citep{wu_pervasive_2011}.

\subsubsection{Target group}
Special target groups (e.g. people with physical disabilities) often require some sort of alternative game to be able to play games at all. For example, deaf people may require extra instructions on the screen and blind people require untraditional ways to interact. A Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) could be a way of bringing games to people that are usually not able to play games. One example is the use of brain waves (via a \href{www.neurosky.com}{NeuroSky} brain set) to control the movement in a Snake game \citep{wang_using_2012}.

\subsubsection{Game genre}
Computer games are generally categorized as one of eight game genres \citep{prensky_fun_2001}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Action Games
    \item Adventure Games
    \item Fighting Games
    \item Puzzle Games
    \item Role-Playing Games (RPG’s)
    \item Simulation Games
    \item Sports Games
    \item Strategy Games
\end{itemize}

However one may define additional categories for games that do not directly belong to any of these genres. Alternative games often consist of special concepts and uncommon gameplay, hence often falling into other categories than the ones mentioned above. An example of a game that may be hard to categorize is Learning Recycling from playing a Kinect Game \citep{ibanez_learning_2015}.

\subsubsection{Domain}
Computer games can be divided into different domains, depending on which purpose they have. Some examples of game domains are entertainment, health, and education. Alternative games may sometimes define their own domains.

\subsubsection{Interaction}
There exists a lot of various interaction forms for games. Examples included cooperation, competition, player vs player (PVP), and more. Interaction can also contain an alternative twist, i.e. interaction forms that are not used in ordinary games. One such concept is the Multiplayer On One Screen Entertainment System (MOOSES) - Players use their own smartphones as controllers for gaming on a shared screen \citep{wang_evaluation_2010}.
\href{http://www.airconsole.com}{AirConsole} is a game console where this concept is utilized. The console includes multiple MOOSES based games.
